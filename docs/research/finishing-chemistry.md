# Darken everything, then rub back the highs

The finish on a laser deep-engraved coin follows one sequence across almost every material: **strip the laser oxide and powder, set the surface texture, darken the whole piece, abrade the patina back off the high points with a tool whose stiffness decides how deep it reaches, then seal**. The chemistry depends on the metal. Brass, bronze and copper use acidic **selenious-acid/copper-sulfate "cold blacks"** (JAX, Birchwood Casey Brass Black, Birchwood Antique Black M20/M24/M38, Sculpt Nouveau). Silver uses **tellurium-dioxide/HCl instant blacks** (Griffith Silver Black, JAX Silver Blackener, Win-Ox) or slow, multi-colour **liver of sulfur**. Carbon steel takes cold gun blues (Perma Blue, Oxpho-Blue). Aluminium takes Birchwood Casey Aluminum Black. Stainless has one verified room-temperature blackener (Birchwood Presto Black SSB). Titanium cannot be chemically blackened and instead takes interference colour from anodizing or heat. For a preview renderer, F0 colours of the bare metals are well documented and agree across three references. Thin-film colours (stainless heat tint, titanium anodizing) can be computed from physics. **Patina colours, roughness-per-finish values and per-tool "reach depth" are not published anywhere these notes found**, so the renderer tables below separate sourced values from calibrated estimates, and the estimates should be tuned against photographed test coins. On the laser question, **1064 nm fiber/MOPA is unsuitable for wood and clear acrylic because both are largely transparent at that wavelength. The 355 nm UV source is the right tool**, and WeCreat's own Lumos Ultra material lists split along exactly that line.

Throughout, **V** marks a value taken from a cited source, **C** a value calculated from sourced inputs, and **E** an estimate made by the research team or this writer that no source supports. Treat E values as starting points for calibration, not facts.

## Six process states drive the preview, from black oxide to sealed relief

### Raw and cleaned surfaces

Coming off a fiber laser, deep relief is not metal-coloured. Thunder Laser notes that "3D metal relief marking produces a lot of powder" and cleans brass coins by brushing under running water with a soft toothbrush, before an optional copper blackening agent ([Thunder Laser](https://www.thunderlaser.com/inspiration-library/laser-marking-brass-coin.html)). Underneath the powder is a tightly bonded oxide. Laser impact reaches **500–800 °C**, forming **CuO on brass and Fe3O4 on stainless**, and vaporised metal recondenses as "fine black particles" on a rough re-melted floor that holds the oxide well. NGL's industrial fix is **40 kHz ultrasound** with proprietary chemistries; the claim that this leaves mirror finishes untouched is vendor marketing ([NGL Group](https://www.ngl-group.com/en/eliminate-oxide-layer-on-stainless-steel-and-brass-after-laser-engraving/)). xTool describes embossed brass as "yellow dust… with a black layer" and removes it with a **magnetic pin tumbler, then an ultrasonic cleaner, then a tap-water rinse** ([xTool](https://support.xtool.com/article/2505)).

Cleaning does not remove texture. A LightBurn user found 3D-carved brass still "sandpaper like" after cleanup, and the fix offered was a lower-power, higher-frequency laser finishing pass ([LightBurn forum](https://forum.lightburnsoftware.com/t/rough-sandpaper-like-finish-on-3d-carved-brass-coin-after-cleanup/142604)). The one measured data point found puts a ns-laser-ablated stainless floor at **Sa 13.8–16.8 µm**, rising with power and pass count ([MDPI Metals 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/15/5/519)). That is far rougher than any polish. If brass is pickled in sodium bisulfate (Sparex) instead of tumbled, zinc oxide dissolves but copper oxide reduces to a **pink-orange copper film** ([Ganoksin](https://www.ganoksin.com/article/technical-briefs-fire-stain-on-brass/)). That source deals with torch fire-stain; that the same happens to laser oxide is an inference. Medal makers deliberately bead- or sand-blast before darkening, leaving "microscopic pits" that hold the solution ([NNP/Johnson: Highlighting](https://nnp.library.wustl.edu/concern/dictionaries/dict516049?locale=en)). So the rough laser floor helps contrast: it holds patina more tenaciously than polished tops.

### Darkening needs bare, grease-free metal

Every chemical source agrees that darkening only works on degreased, oxide-free metal, handled with gloves and blackened straight after cleaning. Patchy results "usually" mean contamination. Full-strength solution gives a "crusty, powdery, or flaky" black, so dilute to **35–50%**. An unrinsed piece "will continue oxidising, even if sealed" ([modelshipbuilder.com](https://modelshipbuilder.com/how-to/blackening-metal.pdf)). JAX adds a Dawn wash as the final degrease and warns that over-dwell makes the finish "rub off the metal easily". Its recommended practice is to **over-darken, then relieve** ([Kibler's Longrifles](https://kiblerslongrifles.com/products/jax-black-darkener-recommended-for-brass-parts)). For the renderer, that confirms the model: patinate the whole surface, then subtract it from the highs. Do not paint patina only into the recesses.

## Selenium blacks dominate copper alloys; tellurium and sulfide own silver

### Copper alloys: one chemistry under many labels

The SDS compositions show that most cold blacks for brass, bronze and copper are the same thing at different strengths. **JAX Black** is nitric acid <1%, selenious acid <1% and copper sulfate 0.5–2.5%, at pH <2 ([JAX Black SDS](https://jaxchemical.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/JAX-Black-SDS.pdf)). **Birchwood Casey Brass Black** is stronger: phosphoric acid 7–13%, selenious acid 1–5%, cupric sulfate, ammonium molybdate and zinc sulfate, at **pH 0.99**, rated "Toxic if swallowed" ([Brass Black SDS](https://www.birchwoodcasey.com/content/datasheets/Brass%20Black%20Touch-Up.pdf)). Birchwood's industrial **Antique Black M24** is essentially the same formula. It is brushed on neat for black, or diluted 10–50% for browns, and meets the US10B oil-rubbed-bronze architectural finish ([Birchwood Technologies](https://www.birchwoodtechnologies.com/traditional-patinas/)). Jewellers report M24 gives "deep greenish browns to blacks on brass", slightly cooler than liver of sulfur ([Ganoksin Orchid](https://orchid.ganoksin.com/t/antiquing-brass/9759)). Liver of sulfur is unreliable on brass: Wikipedia says brass "does not react with it" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liver_of_sulfur)), though one jeweller says it works fine. For brass coins, the selenium blacks are the dependable cold route.

Sculptors' patinas extend the palette. **Sculpt Nouveau Traditional Antiquing** gives dark brown to black on copper alloys in **3–5 minutes at 50–80 °F**, lighter when diluted ([Sculpt Nouveau TDS](https://potterycrafts.co.uk/cdn/shop/files/tds-traditional-antiquing-web-202603.pdf)). The classic hot recipes give translucent browns and blue-greens, but torching a thin coin risks warping, so hot recipes suit sculpture better than coins (an inference). Sources: ferric nitrate (browns, "build up slowly due to transparency") and cupric nitrate (blue-green) ([David M Bowman Studio](https://davidmbowman.com/patinas/formulas.htm)). Two hot immersion recipes have real timings. **Thiosulfate plus ferric nitrate at 50–60 °C** goes purplish in about 45 s and black by about 1 min. **Potassium permanganate at 90 °C** gives a golden lustre within a minute ([Science Company](https://www.sciencecompany.com/Patina-Formulas-for-Brass-Bronze-and-Copper.aspx)).

### Silver: instant tellurium black versus a controllable sulfide rainbow

**Griffith Silver Black** lists hydrochloric acid and tellurium(IV) oxide and gives an "instantaneous black patina" ([Otto Frei](https://www.ottofrei.com/products/griffith-silver-black-oxidizer-16-oz)). JAX Silver Blackener is reported to be the same chemistry. On sterling it takes "only… a few seconds", so quench at once in fresh water ([Stones and Findings](https://stonesandfindings.com/using-jax-to-antique-sterling-silver/)). It also darkens copper, **nickel silver** and brass; neutralise in baking soda water (3 tbsp per cup) ([Beaducation](https://beaducation.com/blogs/beaducation-blog/silver-black-perfect-for-oxidizing-metal-and-darkening-impressions)). **Liver of sulfur** runs a documented interference sequence on silver: **gold, pink, magenta, blue, purple, bronze, gray, black**. Heat speeds it up, cold water stops it, and warm (not hot) solution gives subtler colours ([Ganoksin](https://www.ganoksin.com/article/liver-of-sulfur-101/)). Ammonia makes the colours brighter ([Fire Mountain Gems](https://www.firemountaingems.com/learn/categories/essential-resources/how-tos/6906-project-tutorial.html)). No source gives seconds per colour stage, so any time-to-colour slider is an estimate. Against abrasion, practitioners rank **JAX Ultra Black > Silver Blackener > liver of sulfur**; 000 steel wool removed Silver Blackener completely ([Ganoksin Orchid](https://orchid.ganoksin.com/t/jax-blackeners/69420)). That ranking is what a renderer's relieve step should respect.

### Stainless, titanium, aluminium and steel follow different rules

Stainless resists the copper-alloy chemistry. **Presto Black SSB** is the only verified room-temperature option. It works only after abrasive or media-blast "activation", which a freshly ablated recess needs anyway ([Birchwood Technologies](https://birchwoodtechnologies.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/Presto-Black-SSB-Inst.pdf)). The ordinary Presto Black is documented for iron and steel only ([Gear Technology](https://geartechnology.com/articles/24085)). Heat on stainless gives temper colours, not black. Heat Treat Today puts the sequence from about 20–30 nm of oxide (straw) through bronze, peacock and blue to grey, and **above about 100 nm "dark blue/gray to black"** ([Heat Treat Today](https://www.heattreattoday.com/?p=69814)). Titanium cannot be blackened at all. Anodizing "cannot produce red or black colors" ([Blade HQ](https://www.bladehq.com/blog/how-to-anodize-titanium)), so a black recess on titanium needs a fill. One idea, inferred rather than sourced, is to anodize the whole part and relieve the highs back to grey metal, which gives coloured recesses against silver highs. Aluminium is the easy one: **Birchwood Casey Aluminum Black** is explicitly marketed for "name plates, plaques, trophies and other engraved items" ([Livens](https://www.livens.co.uk/product/birchwood-casey-aluminum-black/)). On carbon steel, Oxpho-Blue is rubbed on with No. 0 steel wool, so blackening and burnishing happen in the same motion ([Brownells](https://shop.brownells.com/tools-cleaning/paint-metal-prep/metal-bluing/oxpho-blue/)).

### Safety that the preview should surface

The selenium blacks are corrosive (pH <1 to 2.2), acutely toxic by ingestion, toxic to aquatic life and **must go to hazardous waste** under EPA codes D002 (corrosive) and D010 (selenium). Required PPE is nitrile or neoprene gloves, a face shield or glasses with side shields, an apron, and exhaust ventilation ([Brass Black SDS](https://www.birchwoodcasey.com/content/datasheets/Brass%20Black%20Touch-Up.pdf); [JAX Black SDS](https://jaxchemical.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/JAX-Black-SDS.pdf)). Japan's GHS classification for selenious acid adds **H372, nervous-system damage from prolonged exposure** ([NITE](https://www.nite.go.jp/chem/english/ghs/06-imcg-0422e.html)). Liver of sulfur decomposes to **hydrogen sulfide**, so the SDS calls for a fume hood and says nothing should go down drains ([LabAlley SDS](https://media.laballey.com/docbuilder/potash-sulfurated-liver-of-sulfur-safety-data-sheet-671bfad2cc776.pdf)). The tellurium/HCl blacks carry Prop 65 warnings. One hazard was not stated verbatim in any SDS but follows from the decomposition data: **acid blackeners and sulfide solutions must never share a container or waste stream, because acid plus sulfide releases H2S**. A per-product badge in DepthView ("Se / corrosive / hazwaste", "H2S / ventilate", "HCl + Te", "lethal voltage") would carry this information without cluttering the preview.

## Tool stiffness, not grit, decides how deep relieving reaches

The single most useful finding for a "buff aggressiveness" control is qualitative but consistent. **Stiff, napless backings stay on the tops and leave patina even in shallow lows. Conformable tools follow the surface down and strip it.** A Ganoksin jeweller put it directly: with "any sort of soft or fluffy buff… the nap of the buff is going to reach down into shallow crevices". Chamois and suede behave the same way, while a hard veg-tanned leather strop is the "best bet" for highs only ([Ganoksin Orchid](https://orchid.ganoksin.com/t/removing-patina-from-a-finished-piece/58781)). Wheel construction tells the same story. Tightly stitched or felt wheels give "precise edge definition", and loose canton flannel "conforms more readily to curved or irregular surfaces" ([Skyjems](https://skyjems.ca/pages/encyclopedia-buffing-wheel)). 3M says its radial bristle discs "produce uniform finishes around protrusions, detail grooves… and other hard to reach areas", so they are high-reach tools ([3M flyer](https://multimedia.3m.com/mws/media/2560038O/scotch-brite-precision-radial-bristle-brush-flyer.pdf)). The industrial medallic version is a pumice slurry on a wet wheel after ammonium sulphide blackening ([NNP/Johnson](https://nnp.library.wustl.edu/concern/dictionaries/dict516049?locale=en)). Hobo-nickel carvers sit at the other extreme: pink or two-colour erasers for "controlled highlight work on specific zones", plus rottenstone, pumice, brass brushes and 0000 steel wool ([Engraver's Cafe](https://engraverscafe.com/goto/post?id=154883)).

Grit then sets how fast patina comes off where the tool touches, and what the bare metal looks like afterwards. Practitioners use **00 steel wool "in one direction only"** for matte satin, 600-grit wet-and-dry for "a soft appearance", felt with rouge for bright highs, and steel brushes for a "shiny blue-black" ([Ganoksin Orchid: Antique satin](https://orchid.ganoksin.com/t/antique-satin/7284)). Pro Polishing pads strip oxidation and leave a "really high shine" ([Metal Clay UK](https://www.metalclay.co.uk/pro-polishing-pads-a-must-have-for-jewellery-makers/)). The Otto Frei 3M jewellery bristle line runs **yellow 80, white 120, red 220, blue 400, pink pumice, peach 6 µm, light green 1 µm** ([Otto Frei](https://ottofrei.com/products/3m-2-radial-bristle-discs)). The industrial Scotch-Brite line uses different colour codes (orange is 80+), so colour alone does not identify a grade ([3M](https://multimedia.3m.com/mws/media/2560038O/scotch-brite-precision-radial-bristle-brush-flyer.pdf)). Commercial antique challenge coins end with matte highs, and "you are not able to achieve a high polished look" ([Lapel Pins Plus](https://lapelpinsplus.com/2010/11/antique-challenge-coins-giving-coins-an-older-classic-look)).

### Proposed buff model (all numbers E)

Treat each tool as a probe with three parameters. **Probe radius r** sets which recesses the tool can bridge. **Compliance depth d_reach** sets how far below the local envelope the tool still makes contact. **Cut rate k** is patina removed per unit dwell where contact occurs. Compute the envelope as a morphological closing of the height map with a disc of radius r. Then compute depth below envelope, δ = h − closing_r(h). Contact weight is w = smoothstep(−d_reach, −0.25·d_reach, δ), scaled by a convexity/pressure term so peaks and edges go first. Patina coverage after relieving is c = c0 · exp(−k · w · dwell). This reproduces the sourced order: sharp peaks and edges first, then broad plateaus, upper walls and wide shallow fields, with narrow deep crevices and inside corners last. It also captures the sourced asymmetry that **wide shallow fields survive rigid tools but not soft buffs**. Pressure should raise d_reach for soft tools, and dwell should round convex edges, leaving a soft bright band (≈0.05–0.2 mm, roughness ≈0.3–0.4).

## Metal reflectance is well documented; patina colour is not

### F0 for polished metals

Three independent sets derived from measured complex refractive indices agree well for silver, aluminium, gold, copper and nickel. The physicallybased.info set is the most complete, covering brass, stainless, zinc and iron as well ([Physically Based](https://physicallybased.info/)). It agrees with Lagarde's filmetrics-derived values ([Lagarde](https://seblagarde.wordpress.com/2011/08/17/feeding-a-physical-based-lighting-mode/)) and Hoffman's SIGGRAPH 2013 table ([Hoffman 2013](https://blog.selfshadow.com/publications/s2013-shading-course/hoffman/s2013_pbs_physics_math_notes.pdf)) to within about ±0.1 on the grey metals. Shade thick, opaque patinas as metallic = 0 with these albedos, blended against the metal **at the BSDF level by coverage**, not by averaging colours. ### Sealers change gloss and colour in predictable directions

Renaissance Wax is a microcrystalline/polyethylene wax developed at the British Museum ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_Wax); [CAMEO](https://cameo.mfa.org/wiki/Renaissance_wax)). One practitioner reports it takes patina "back at least one color" and "severely lessens the color intensity", so some makers over-darken to compensate. Lacquer is glossier and holds colour better ([Ganoksin Orchid](https://orchid.ganoksin.com/t/renaissance-wax/7507)). **Incralac** is an acrylic with benzotriazole inhibitor in toluene, developed by INCRA for copper and bronze ([Rupert Harris](https://rupertharris.com/products/incralac)). Practitioners found Sculpt Nouveau Clear Guard satin "identical" to an uncoated hand-rubbed satin, and ProtectaClear effective but with mixed reactions to its look ([Ganoksin Orchid](https://orchid.ganoksin.com/t/preserving-copper-and-bronze/60901)). In the renderer (E): wax lowers roughness on rough areas by about 0.1 and lightens or desaturates the patina by roughly 15–25%. Gloss lacquer adds a clearcoat layer (F0 0.04, roughness 0.05–0.15) over everything, recesses included. Satin lacquer adds a clearcoat at roughness 0.3–0.45 with negligible colour change. No manufacturer gloss-unit values were found for any of these products.

## UV engraves wood and acrylic; 1064 nm mostly passes through

**A 1064 nm fiber or MOPA source cannot reliably engrave wood or clear acrylic**, because "wood and clear plastics… are largely transparent at that wavelength" ([xTool](https://www.xtool.com/blogs/xtool-academy/types-of-laser-engravers-explained)). WeCreat's own Lumos Ultra MOPA list (brass, stainless, aluminium, titanium, silver, gold, nickel, slate, coated metals and similar) contains **no wood and no acrylic**. Its 6 W UV list includes **basswood, plywood, MDF, oak, walnut, maple, bamboo and acrylic** ([WeCreat Lumos Ultra](https://wecreat.com/en-ca/pages/lumos-ultra-6w-uv-60w-100w-mopa-laser-engraver)). At 355 nm, "each photon carries enough energy to break molecular bonds directly rather than heating the material" ([xTool](https://www.xtool.com/blogs/xtool-academy/types-of-laser-engravers-explained)). A UV source maker describes PMMA processing at that wavelength as non-thermal "photo-etching" ([RFH](https://www.rfhtech.com/blog/355nm-uv-nanosecond-laser-source-for-acrylic-cutting-and-engraving_b3537)), though that text markets 30 W+ industrial sources.

The sources disagree on depth. xTool rates UV as "engrave only" on wood and acrylic. WeCreat claims cuts **up to 15 mm in wood and 12 mm in black acrylic**, with pass count and time unstated. The two are reconcilable if WeCreat's figures are many slow passes, so expect 6 W UV deep relief in wood or acrylic to be slow and plan relief depths accordingly. WeCreat quotes a depth only for black acrylic, which hints that clear acrylic is harder even at 355 nm. Pigment may let MOPA mark dark woods or black acrylic somewhat. Both points are inferences no source states. For fills, the sourced wood workflow is **mask → engrave → seal with water-based polycrylic → paint-fill (heavy-body acrylic needs fewer coats) → peel → light sand**. Oil finishes should never go on before lasering, because they can catch fire ([Handyman's Daughter](https://www.thehandymansdaughter.com/paint-fill-laser-engraving/)). Without a mask, overfill and sand back, which is the wood equivalent of relieving a patina. Epoxy, wax-stick, mica and acrylic-specific fill-and-polish steps were not sourced.

## Conclusion

The finishing problem reduces to one rendering primitive used twice. A coverage mask, computed as depth below a tool-dependent envelope, decides both where patina survives relieving and where wax or lacquer pools. Chemistry mostly chooses a palette and a durability constant. The four tone families are selenium blacks/browns, tellurium instant blacks, sulfide interference-to-black, and cupric greens. Thin films (anodizing, heat tint, MOPA colour marking) form a separate class that changes the metal's own reflectance rather than covering it, and the calculated thickness lookup table handles them from physics instead of guesswork. The weakest links are empirical: patina albedos, finish roughness, and tool reach depths are all estimates. They are, however, few in number and low-dimensional, so a small calibration set closes the gap. That set is a handful of laser-relief test coins in brass, copper and stainless, each darkened with one product and relieved with three tools of known stiffness, photographed under controlled light. A MOPAChroma Capture-style rig is well suited to collect it.

A second implication concerns process order rather than rendering. The laser floor's 14–17 µm roughness and recast oxide work against an even patina but in favour of contrast after relieving. A preview that lets users toggle "pickle / tumble / blast before darkening" will predict real outcomes better than one that varies only the chemical. The practitioner record consistently blames uneven results on surface preparation rather than on which product was used.
